2. **A DECISION** Pavel rides into Nightmarch at noon, bone-weary after three days in the saddle plus battles against trolls, satyrs, and his own indecision. Riven, at the head of the column, puts on a facade of cheerfulness by greeting people companionably, exchanging jokes and making lighthearted promises. He extends a hand to Linzi, who swings lightly up onto his horse’s back and sits behind him, bringing him up to speed on news of the city. Pavel notices, however, how Riven’s eyes scan the back of every crowd and every open window, looking for something he doesn’t expect to see but can’t stop hoping for. Behind Riven, the companions are a decidedly glum, tired lot. The nearby citizens put up a nervous cheer, but it doesn’t last long when the companions fail to respond. Pavel suspects they know how bad the situation is in the countryside, and how thin their defenses. At last the stable comes into view. Pavel gratefully turns his horse over to the groomsmen, wondering once again when he had become so high and mighty that he couldn’t take care of his own animals. Shaking his head at himself, he stretches his aching back, grabs his pack, and turns gratefully toward home.
    
3. Alone at last inside the cheap room he rents up against the city walls, Pavel finds himself unable to rest or sleep. He paces the floor, thinking about that morning three days ago when Edina met him by the stables. _What did it mean? How was he supposed to respond?_He had assumed Astor would be able to interpret it all, but the big man had been no help at all. Finally, unable to bear the tension any longer, he flops on the floor and fishes underneath the bed for the present Linzi gave him several years ago. He pulls out a small box filled with writing utensils: pieces of parchment, sharpened quills, a candle, and a stick of sealing wax, all unused. He unstoppers the ink bottle, peers inside, and swishes it around. Still liquid! He lifts the box and carries it to the small table against the wall. He pulls out a piece of parchment, dips the quill in the ink and carefully scratches out a brief message, frowning in concentration and sounding out the words as he writes. **MEET ME AT THE REISENSTEIN TONIGHT. – PAVEL** Quickly now, as if afraid someone will materialize and talk him out of it, he folds the paper into thirds and writes EDINA ALDORI on the backside. Then he heats up the sealing wax in the flame of the candle as he’s seen Linzi do many times, dribbling hot wax over the letter where the page ends meet. He doesn’t have a sealing ring, so after a moment’s thought, he pulls out Cat’s magical figurine and presses the cat’s face into the wax. He pulls it out, leaving an indentation in the wax that looks nothing like Cat, shrugs, and pockets the letter. He releases Cat from his magical figurine and then, with determination and no small amount of fear, they set off together in search of a messenger.
    
4. **AT THE REISENSTEIN** Alone in a booth, Pavel sits, and stares, and thinks about a dead woman. He’s been here for nearly three hours, saying nothing, greeting no one, and drinking only small beer. The well-dressed proprietor of the Reisenstein has gently steered customers to other locations on the ground floor: preferably, locations that weren’t within sight of the very quiet, very deadly man in the booth. In Pavel’s palm, he holds an open locket. He doesn’t pull it out very often these days, but he’d wanted it close by tonight. Inside, the locket has two paintings: On the left, his mother looks out at him as she must have appeared in the prime of her youth: vibrant, mischievous, and effortlessly beautiful. The painting appears to have been done by a master Elven craftsman. But from the look in her eyes, she’s clearly not looking at _him_, Pavel. She must be imagining a very different man; but who? On the right, his mother looks as he remembers her from his youth: Careworn, sad, but putting on a brave face. The two pictures could not have been many years apart – certainly not for an elf – but she appears to have aged a decade or more in that time. The second painting is simpler, done by a more modest painter. But Pavel likes this painting more than the other one, for this is a face he recognizes: the smile, the sad eyes, and the love. Not for the first time, he wonders if she had known she was dying when she sat for the second portrait. He suspects so. She gave him the locket just a week before she died, and told him to remember her by it. In his youth and ignorance, he hadn’t recognized that she was telling him goodbye. But he clutched it tightly for 24 hours after her final breath, and – along with the clothes on his back – it was the only thing he took with him when the man from the castle came and fetched him to the kennels. _What would she have told me to do tonight?_, he wonders. But her portrait provides no answers.

AT THE DOOR

  Pavel can't catch all of it from the booth, but he catches enough. The  proprietor is doing the thing well-bred proprietors do when they mean to maintain the standard of their place. "I'm sorry Miss, I'm afraid I have to insist..."  Although he can't see anything but the top of her hair, Pavel instantly recognizes the voice.  Pavel is sure he knows how this encounter will go — he's watched a hundred highborn win a hundred doors,  and it always finishes with a name dropped on the counter like a coin: Do you know who I am. He waits for it. It doesn't  come. Whatever she's saying, low and fierce, she is winning the argument not as an Aldori, but as a nobody — and something that has been clenched tight in Pavel's chest for weeks quietly lets go. She's not pulling  rank. She could, and she won't. He hasn't the words for why that undoes him. He only knows that the woman he's spent months  believing stood a hundred rungs above him is in the doorway refusing to stand on a single one.

  Then the proprietor steps aside —  and  she comes in.

  And Pavel stops thinking about doors.  Edina is in her leather riding gear with her bow and an empty quiver slung behind her and an aldori sword at her hip.  She is filthy, but most urgently to Pavel, she is hurt.

  He's on his feet before he decides to be. The tankard rocks; the folded, terrified man who's been rehearsing sentences for  three hours is simply gone, and in his place is the field medic who spent eight years on the borders and reads a body the way  he reads a wounded animal — at a glance, without asking. Torn sleeve at the shoulder. A dark stain gone stiff and brown, not
  fresh, which means she's been bleeding for hours and never stopped to tend it. Smoke in her hair. A shallow line along her  jaw she hasn't even noticed. She favors the left. He crosses to her the way he'd cross to a snared deer — steady, unhurried,  hands already open.  She starts to say "Pavel, I just got in and saw your note, I.." — but he interrupts, not with any of the words he's been practicing: "You're bleeding. Sit down. Let me see it."

  She sits.  His hands are already at the kit on his belt, easing the ruined sleeve  back from a gash that runs the length of her forearm — long, not deep, ugly with dried blood and road-dust. Claw. Not a blade.  The proprietor — resigned to having his tavern serve as a medic ward — sets down clean water and a  stack of linen and retreats; Pavel never notices him.  He wets the cloth. "This will sting. Talk to me. What happened."

  And she does — not the way the highborn tell a story, all shape and flourish, but flat and in order, the way a soldier tells  it, while he cleans the grit from the wound.   "Three days back", she says, "word came down from the steadings north of the city — something had come out of the treeline. Big. Wrong. The kind of thing the blooms cough up".  "It was the third attack to come up since you left for Oaktop.  Every man who could hold a spear had already marched in one of the groups. Linzi had the news and no notion what to do with it — pacing the war-room with a quill like she could write the thing to death, calling  for riders who weren't there, going pale. She's a marvel with a song and a ledger," Edina says, "and she was drowning.   Someone had to move."

  Of course you did, Pavel thinks. There was never going to be a version where you didn't. He  knows that wiring from the inside. You don't leave your people to it. There's no choosing involved — it isn't courage, it's  just the shape you're built in — and it startles him to find that shape in Edina.  He happened to know that she hadn't been out on a  hunt since Jolia had died.

 But she moved. She gathered who was left, and who was left was women: the fletcher's wife who could put an arrow through a  knot at forty paces, two of the militia's daughters, an old huntress with a grudge and a boar-spear, a handful more. She led them out, swallowing back the fear that some of these women might meet the same fate as her sister. "The last time I rode out to kill something like that," she says, "I went home alone." Pavel's hands go still on the cloth. "This time, we all made it."

  Then she turns it on him and asks if it's true: a group they met on the return road said that Pavel charged a fey general the size of a house, alone, at Oaktop.  Pavel shakes his head and let's out a sigh.  "You should have seen this team's strategies"   He tells her about the Ankou: thirty feet of night and fire, how it generaled the ambush, the terrible discipline of  the satyrs, how you don't so much fight a thing like that as refuse to let it fight you on its terms. She leans in. She asks  the right questions — where were its blind spots, how did you close the distance, what does a thing made of fire do when you get inside its reach — how many were you, did you flank?
  
  Twenty minutes in, Pavel realizes, with something close to vertigo,  that this is _easy_.   It catches up with him somewhere in the binding of her bruised ribs — winding the linen the way his hands have wound a thousand times before as he's grousing about swinging at the Ankou and missing so completely that he stumbles and falls right on top of one of the fallen satyrs, making her laugh (and then wince at the pain of laughing with bruised ribs). The conversation just _flows_.  And she's close.  He can see the pale thread of an old  scar at her hairline, smell the smoke and horse and iron-tang of the fight still on her, and under it, warm, her. When she  answers him, her breath stirs the loose hair around her face, and he's aware of it the way he's aware of everything — the  animal accounting he can't switch off — except the accounting keeps arriving at conclusions it has no business reaching. She is beautiful. Not the way she was beautiful at the feast, lit and laughing and a hundred rungs away — beautiful here,  bloodied and spent and  telling him about the worst kind of day in the flattest, bravest voice he's ever heard.
